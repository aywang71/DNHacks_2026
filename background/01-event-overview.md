## Who runs it, and why that matters

DNHacks is run by **The David Network**, which describes itself as "a community of over 5,000 conservative students, alumni, and professionals who drive change in America's leading institutions & industries," grounded in "conservative ideas, grounded in the Biblical moral tradition, embodied through limited, constitutional government."

The hackathon's own framing on the site:

> "For 20 years, the strongest engineers and technical minds at top schools have been pulled into SaaS, consumer internet, and finance, and away from the industries the country most needs them in."

> "Our mission is to catalyze a fundamental reprioritization of what America's top technical talent chooses to build, setting the focus on technologies that will build the future of American industrial, military, and technological dominance."

> "We want our members to pioneer world-class technology built on the foundation of American exceptionalism, freedom, and unusual ambition."

The site's hero rotates between "Build the future of **American Activism**" and "**American Defense**."

**Why this matters for us:** this is not a neutral technical audience. The organizing frame is explicitly American-industrial-revival and national-security-forward. Projects that frame themselves in terms of national capability, sovereignty, taxpayer cost, or institutional capacity will read as on-mission. Projects framed primarily as consumer products, or as critiques of US institutions, will read as off-mission. This is about *framing*, not about changing what we build — the same technical work can be presented either way, and the on-mission framing is free.

**Venue context:** Station DC is a 13,000 sq ft non-profit clubhouse whose stated mission is to "connect innovators, investors, government and military leaders to build the future in DC." The room will be thick with defense-tech operators, VCs, and federal officials — not academics.

## Rules and constraints that affect what we build

- **AI is allowed, but discounted if it's all you have.** Verbatim: *"Yes, you can use AI to help build your project, but judges will heavily weight the technical depth that you bring to your project."* Expect judges to probe for what *we* contributed beyond prompting.
- **Every participant gets 2,500 Codex credits ($100) from OpenAI.** OpenAI is the AI sponsor of record, with four staff on the guest list and the opening keynote.
- **Team cap is 5.**
- **Judging is multi-pass.** *"Each category and special award will have its own set of judges. Plan to be judged by three groups of judges during project demos."* The showcase is a repeated-pitch format, not a single stage presentation.
- **Two-stage final.** Showcase at 1:00 p.m., then finalists do **live demos** at 4:00 p.m. The demo must survive being run twice, hours apart, on venue wifi.
- **Prize eligibility is gated.** *"Only winners and runners up from the category prizes will be eligible to win the overall prize and runner up."* You must place in a category to compete for the top award. Special awards (Best in Design, Best Use of AI) can go to any project.
- **Participant's Choice is peer-voted** — legibility to fellow students matters, independent of judges.

## What organizers say they want from participants

> "Strong engineers, designers, and technical developers · Students interested in systems-level impact · Builders who want real agency instead of busywork"

And on what follows the weekend — top participants are *"Connected to high-level public and private sector projects · Invited to continue building with curated teams · Considered for internships, fellowships, and grant funding."* The overall prize is structured as $5,000 cash + a $20,000 grant **conditional on continued work**, which tells you the organizers are selecting for projects that can plausibly continue past Sunday. A project with an obvious next 6 months is worth more to them than a finished toy.
